Making the card look tappable

The card opens a detail sheet, but nothing on it says so. Google's tappability study found people routinely misjudge whether a flat element is interactive — and the fix is a signifier, a visible mark, not just the affordance itself. Our diner is often 45+, on a borrowed phone, in a dim room, and has no Swiggy habit to fall back on. Three ways to say it, from quietest to loudest.

Option A · Chevron + lift

Cost: shadows on every row make a long menu feel busy, and the chevron competes with the offer flag for the same corner.

Option A, plus a one-time coach tip on the first card of a diner's first visit, with a small horizontal nudge. Dismissed on the first tap and never shown again (localStorage).

Cost: real state to store and a tooltip to position; wasted on the regular who scans the same menu twice a week.

My pick: A, and only A. The chevron is the cheapest honest signal and it survives a 60-item menu. B is louder but the phrase repeats down the whole page, and it needs Tamil to be fair to half your diners. C is the right answer if you find in testing that people genuinely do not tap — but it is state and a tooltip for a problem a chevron may already solve, so it is worth measuring before building. Whichever you pick, the card also needs a pressed state — a brief scale(0.985) — because on a phone there is no hover, and the press is the only confirmation the tap registered.
